Importing from China to sell on eBay UK

What you actually pay to get stock into the UK, and how to price it so you still make money.

This is a plain-English summary, not tax advice. Rules and rates change, so check the GOV.UK and eBay pages linked below, or ask an accountant, before making big decisions.

1. Landed cost: the real cost of your stock

The price on the supplier's site is only the start. Your landed cost is everything you pay to get one unit into your hands in the UK:

  • the item price
  • your share of shipping and insurance
  • customs duty
  • import VAT, unless you're VAT registered and can reclaim it

Shipping is usually charged per shipment, so ordering more units at once spreads it more thinly. Put the shipment's size into the calculator's "Units in shipment" field to see the effect.

2. The £135 rule

The value of the goods in a shipment, not counting shipping and insurance, decides how it's taxed:

  • £135 or less: no customs duty (except on excise goods like alcohol, tobacco and vapes). The seller or marketplace adds VAT at checkout, so there's nothing to pay the courier.
  • Over £135: the courier collects import VAT and any duty before handing over the parcel, often with a handling fee of their own on top.

Small test orders often fall under £135. Once you scale up to bigger shipments, duty and import VAT start applying, so re-run your numbers when you start ordering in bulk.

3. Customs duty and commodity codes

Duty rates depend on what the product is. Every product type has a commodity code, and each code has its own rate, from 0% for many items to 12% or more for some clothing and footwear. Look up your product on the UK Trade Tariff to find its code and rate.

Duty is charged on the goods plus shipping and insurance:

Duty = (goods + shipping + insurance) × duty rate

4. Import VAT and VAT registration

Import VAT is charged on the goods, shipping and insurance plus the duty, usually at 20%:

Import VAT = (goods + shipping + insurance + duty) × 20%

Whether you can get it back depends on whether you're VAT registered:

  • Not registered: import VAT is a straight cost. You don't charge VAT on your sales either.
  • Registered: you can reclaim import VAT, and you can use postponed VAT accounting to avoid paying it upfront. But you must also pay VAT on every sale: 1/6 of a VAT-inclusive price at 20%.

You must register once your taxable turnover goes over £90,000 in any 12 months. Turnover means sales, not profit, so a busy reseller on thin margins can hit it quickly. Tick both VAT boxes in the calculator to see how registration changes your profit.

5. eBay fees

If you buy stock to resell, eBay treats you as a business seller. You'll typically pay:

  • Final value fee: a percentage of the total the buyer pays, including any postage you charge. The rate depends on the category.
  • Per-order fee: a small fixed amount on each order.
  • Promoted listings: an optional extra percentage, if you choose to promote listings.

Check the current rates for your category on eBay's business selling fees page.

6. Pricing for a target margin

Most eBay fees are a percentage of your price, so you can't just add your margin on top of cost. Divide your fixed costs by what's left after the percentage cuts instead:

Min price = (landed + postage + packaging + fixed fee) ÷ (1 − fee % − promoted % − sale VAT share − target %)

The calculator does this for you. If fees, VAT and your target margin add up to 100% or more, no price can reach that margin, so you'd need to lower your target or your costs.
